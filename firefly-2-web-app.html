On iPhone — Safari + beacio (alternative)

Plain Safari can't do Web Bluetooth on its own, but the free beacio extension (search the App Store for "ioswebble") adds it. One-time setup:

  1. Install beacio from the App Store and open it once.

  3. Enable it in Safari: tap the Page Menu at the left of the address bar → Manage Extensions → turn beacio on.
  4. Back on this page, tap beacio in the address bar → Enable BLE → "Always allow on this site".
  5. Reload this page, then tap Connect.

How the numbers are made

The 58% RH assumption

All strain temperatures assume flower stored at 58% RH (water activity ≈ 0.58 — inside the ASTM D8197-22 safe band of 0.55–0.65). No temperature adjustment is applied for moisture: no peer-reviewed dry-herb-vaporizer study of moisture exists to base one on.

Suggested starting temperature — a flavour/extraction heuristic, not a scientific optimum

Each strain's range starts from its documented top-3 terpenes: 185°C if all three are volatile monoterpenes, 190°C for a mixed profile containing caryophyllene, 195°C if linalool or humulene is in the top-3 or the strain is CBD/balanced — floored at 180°C (extraction is poor below ~170°C) and capped at 210°C (efficient-delivery zone, below the pyrolysis-approach zone). Shown as a range (base −5/+10°C); start low for flavour, finish higher for fuller extraction.

Limits: terpene listings are database averages — your actual batch may differ; a lab COA for the batch overrides this dataset. Device error and gradients can exceed the 5–10°C steps here. Sesquiterpene boiling points (~256–275°C) are deliberately not targeted — chasing them needs destructive heat; they still evaporate at 185–205°C.

The famous 157°C "THC boiling point" is a vacuum value

157°C was measured at 0.05 Torr (Adams 1941; Gaoni & Mechoulam 1964) — vacuum distillation, not a normal boiling point. The best atmospheric estimate is 245 ± 6°C (disputed; an EPA model predicts 375°C). Vaporizers work by evaporation below the boiling point — THC shows measurable vapour pressure from ~91–151°C — and measured extraction rises from ~22–24% at 170°C to ~71–77% at 230°C. That curve, not a boiling chart, is what guides this app.

Burn Guard calibration

Three layers of protection against scorched flower: a hard ceiling for flower, setpoints adjusted to how your unit actually runs, and the device's own coil calibration. The temperature you set is the maximum a draw can reach.

1 · Flower ceiling

The hottest temperature Flower mode will send, full stop. Anything above it is refused with an explanation. Concentrates mode is fenced off separately and isn't capped by this.

380°F

Step up, don't jump. The gentlest flower sessions start low — around 320–350°F, where the flavor compounds release — and climb 10–20°F at a time between draws, finishing hotter for full extraction. Because the set temperature is the draw's maximum, stepping up keeps every stage under your ceiling instead of sitting at it.

The honest limit

Burn Guard can't watch your bowl in real time. The Firefly's settings reports carry no live temperature reading — there is no temperature field in them at all — so no app can see the coil overshoot and cut power mid-draw. There is no closed loop here.

What protects your flower is the combination above: a hard ceiling, setpoints compensated by your own measurements, and the coil calibration. The heat-up ramp itself — how fast the temperature climbs during a draw — is baked into the device's firmware and cannot be changed from any app.

How solid is this?

Compounds actually start releasing below their boiling points, so every temperature here says "starts releasing around." The boiling points are lab measurements; the experiences are what people commonly report — not medical claims.

The popular "entourage effect" — compounds boosting each other — is not proven science yet. One small early human trial hints limonene may take the edge off THC anxiety, and β-caryophyllene is known to act on the body's CB2 receptors in the lab — both are early, mechanism-level evidence, not guarantees.

Two numbers on many charts are misleading: β-caryophyllene (~266°F) and humulene (~222°F) were measured under vacuum. At normal air pressure they boil around 493–527°F (~256–275°C) — above this device's range — so they largely remain in the bowl at flower temperatures, releasing gradually on the way up rather than switching on at those low numbers.

The famous THC “boiling point” of 157°C / 315°F is a 0.05 Torr vacuum-distillation value (1941/1964), not an atmospheric boiling point — the atmospheric estimate is disputed (245 ± 6°C vs a 375°C model). That is why this chart shows THC and CBD as gradual extraction across a range, not a single switch: measured extraction rises from ~22–24% at 170°C to ~71–77% at 230°C across studies.

    What this can and can't know

    The Firefly reports only its 5-step gauge — no health, voltage, or cycle data exists to read. This tracker estimates health from how long each gauge step lasts during real use, and it can only observe while this app is connected — time on the Firefly with the app closed is invisible to it, so totals are observed minutes, not the battery's whole life between charges.

    Cold weather and heavier draws shorten gauge steps on any healthy battery, so a low reading after a cold, hard session may say more about the session than the pack. Treat the estimate as a trend, not a lab measurement.

    And the baseline is your own early performance with this pack — the median of your first 3 completed Full → Redline runs — not a factory specification. Swap in a fresh removable pack and use “New battery — reset baseline” so the new pack is judged against itself.
