Forecast

Swell, period and wind for the six cam breaks — Open-Meteo Marine + Forecast models, no account, no key.

How the 0–100 score works (nothing hidden)

Swell does most of the talking: height is worth up to 45 pts (full marks at 8 ft) and period up to 30 pts (full marks at 16 s) — long-period groundswell carries more energy than the same height of windswell. Wind is worth 25 pts: it starts at 12.5, gains up to +12.5 when the wind blows offshore (land to sea, grooming the faces) and loses 0.5 pts for every mph above 5 — so light offshore scores best and strong onshore scores worst. It's a simple, published formula, not a pro forecast.

The dawn call

A waiting period is a window, not a promise. Each morning of the window, contest directors check the swell, wind and tides at dawn and call the day on, off, or on hold. Plenty of window days never run — that's the sport working as intended.

Big Wave

The heavy-water circuit: live model conditions, the same charts as Forecast, and an honest XL heuristic.

“The Bay Calls the Day” — the Eddie

The Eddie Aikau Invitational at Waimea Bay has no fixed date. It runs only on a day when Waimea is big enough — historically, waves in the 30 ft-plus range through the whole day — which is why whole winters can pass without it. When the bay calls, the invitees drop everything.

Nazaré Tow-In Challenge

At Praia do Norte, Nazaré, the WSL's tow-in challenge pairs surfers with jet-ski drivers who whip them into waves too fast and steep to paddle. Its window runs through the North Atlantic winter (the 2026/27 window runs to March 31, 2027), and like the Eddie it only runs when the swell truly shows up.

Boards & Shapes

How a board's design translates to behavior on the water.

First, the physics

A surfboard is a planing surface, not a wing. At rest and at paddle speed it's supported by buoyancy — roughly 1 liter of volume floats 1 kg — and planing lift only takes over once the wave gives it speed. So nearly every design choice trades the same two things against each other: planing area, flatness and volume (early lift, paddle power, speed in weak waves) versus curve, reduced area and thin rails/tails (the ability to sink, bite and hold control at speed). Every family and element below is a pre-packaged answer to that one trade.

Straight talk: folklore vs. established

Shapers know what works — but some of the usual explanations don't hold up, and bottom-contour theory in particular is largely trial-and-error with little hard lab evidence behind it (Greenlight and Surf Simply both say so openly).

“Concaves lift you by Bernoulli/Venturi.” Folklore. A board bottom is an open surface, not a closed venturi tube. The best-supported mechanism is that a concave flattens the stringer rocker relative to the rail rocker — flatter planing panels for speed, curvier rails for turning.
“Channels create thrust.” Metaphor, not measurement. Channels direct flow and add hold; no published test isolates a thrust effect, and they can feel tracky.
“Quads are faster.” Only sometimes. CFD work found quads make more lift but also more drag — slower than a thruster across most angles of attack, with their edge at high angles (speed and hold in serious surf). A thruster's best lift-to-drag sits near a 6.25° angle of attack.
“Tail shape X always does Y.” Oversold. In CFD (Oggiano & Pierella, 2018), swapping squash vs. round tail alone changed nothing in the cases analysed; the area and rail-line changes that come with a tail swap likely do most of the work. The same study found more rocker raises both lift and drag.
“More volume is always better.” No. Past what you need, volume fights rail-sinking, duck-diving and hold. 1 L ≈ 1 kg is a flotation shorthand, not a prescription.
“Fins provide most of the grip.” Probably backwards. The inside rail — water wrapping it via the Coanda effect — likely provides most grip in most conditions; fins add lateral resistance and turn control. Finless alaia boards hold and drive on rails alone.
